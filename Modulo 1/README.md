# Module 1 · The Danger

## Attack scenarios
Examples of how and why networks get attacked.
- **Evil twin (fake Wi-Fi hotspot):** an attacker → sets up a fake "free Wi-Fi" in a café → to hijack a victim's session while they use online banking → access to the victim's bank accounts.
- **Ransomware via phishing:** an attacker → sends a fake email posing as the CEO with a malicious PDF → to encrypt company data and demand a ransom → data locked and money demanded from the company.
- **State-sponsored malware (Stuxnet):** nation-state-level attackers → infected USB drives that spread to Windows systems and then Siemens PLCs → to damage the centrifuges of a uranium enrichment facility in Iran → physical damage to critical infrastructure.
- **IoT botnet DDoS (Dyn, October 2016):** attackers → infected webcams, DVRs and routers with malware to form a botnet → to launch a massive DDoS against the DNS provider Dyn → many popular websites went down.

## Threat actors
Who carries out cyberattacks (intentional, malicious acts meant to harm a person or organization) and what motivates them.
- **Amateurs (script kiddies):** little or no skill; they use existing tools or online guides. Some are just curious, others want to show off, but the damage can still be serious.
- **Hacktivists:** attack to support a political or social cause.
- **Organized crime groups:** attack for financial gain.
- **State-sponsored actors:** work for or on behalf of a government, mainly to steal trade secrets and pursue global political goals.
- **Terrorist groups:** attack to push an ideology and cause fear or disruption.

## Impact of threats
The total economic cost of cyberattacks is hard to measure, but estimates put yearly losses for companies in the trillions of dollars (around $5 trillion by 2024).

### Stolen personal data (PII, PHI and PSI)
- **PII (Personally Identifiable Information):** any data that identifies a person: name, date of birth, social security number, ID documents, card and bank account numbers, address, email, phone. Criminals sell it on the dark web and use it to open fake financial accounts, credit cards or loans.
- **PHI (Protected Health Information):** a subset of PII found in electronic medical records. It is regulated by HIPAA in the US and covered by the GDPR in the EU.
- **PSI (Personal Security Information):** usernames, passwords and other data used to access systems and services. According to a 2019 Verizon report, stolen credentials were the second most common way attackers breached networks.
- **Real breaches:** in 2019–2020, three major platforms (an online design tool, a Chinese social network and a Facebook games company) lost the data of 137, 172 and 218 million users respectively.

### Loss of competitive advantage
- Companies worry about corporate espionage and the theft of intellectual property by competitors.
- Losing customers' trust after failing to protect their data can hurt a company more than losing trade secrets.

### Politics and national security
- **Politically motivated leaks:** in February 2016, a hacker published personal data of 20,000 FBI employees and 9,000 DHS employees.
- **Cyberwar:** Stuxnet is the clear example of an attack driven by national security. State-sponsored hackers can disrupt or destroy vital services in an enemy nation, and attacking its online business and finance can damage its economy.
- **Industrial controllers:** similar to those hit by Stuxnet, they also manage water flow in dams and power grids, so attacking them can have severe consequences.

Insecure IoT devices add to the risk: many never get firmware updates, so a single vulnerability can be exploited at scale.

## Key takeaway
Attacks can target people, companies or nations, and the attacker's motivation changes in each case. Even everyday connected devices can become weapons if they are not updated, and the most valuable loss is often trust, not just data.
