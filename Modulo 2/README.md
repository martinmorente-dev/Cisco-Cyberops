# Module 2 Fighters Against Crime

## SOC Elements
A Security Operations Center (SOC) is a team of professionals that defends an organization against current threats with a formal, structured approach. Its services range from monitoring and mangement to full threat-protection solution and hosted security. A SOC can be run in-house or outsourced to a security provider (for example, Cisco Managed Security Services).

Its three main elements are:
- **People:** [who works in a SOC and their roles]
- **Process:** [how the SOC detects and responds to incidents]
- **Technology:** [tools the SOC uses to monitor and defend]

## People in the SOC
The roles in SOC are evolving fast. Nowadays these are the roles of the SOC team:

- **Alert Analyst level 1:** [Monitoring the entry alerts and verify if its a real incident and if it's so resend the tickets to level 2].

- **Incident Response level 2:** [They are responsible of realize a deep investigations of the incidents and to advise solutions or actions.]

- **Threat hunters level3:** [They have an expert level of skills in networking, terminal, threat intelligence and reverse malware enginering.]

- **SOC Administrator:** [They administrate all the resources of the SOC and are the contact point with the rest of the organization]

## Technologies of the SOC - SIEM
A SOC needs a **SIEM (Security Information and Event Management)** system or equivalent. It makes sense of all the data generated by firewalls, network devices, intrusion detection systems and other devices.

A SIEM is used to:
- Collect and filter data.
- Detect and classify threats.
- Analyze and investigate threats.
- Manage resources to apply preventive measures and handle future threats.

SOC technologies cover one or more of these areas:
- **Event collection, correlation and analysis:** gather events from many sources and link them to spot suspicious patterns.
- **Security monitoring:** watch the environment continuously to detect incidents.
- **Security control:** manage the security measures that protect the systems.
- **Log management:** store and organize logs so they can be reviewed later.
- **Vulnerability assessment:** find weaknesses in systems before attackers do.
- **Vulnerability tracking:** follow each weakness until it is fixed.
- **Threat intelligence:** use information about current threats and attackers to anticipate attacks.

## Technologies of the SOC - SOAR
SIEM and SOAR are often paired because their capabilities complement each other. Large security teams (SecOps) use both to optimize their SOC.

SOAR platforms are similar to SIEM in that they collect, correlate and analyze alerts. The difference is that SOAR goes a step further: it integrates threat intelligence and automates investigation and incident-response workflows based on **playbooks** written by the security team.

A SOAR platform:
- Collects alarm data from every component of the system.
- Gives tools to investigate, evaluate and research cases.
- Focuses on integration to automate response workflows for complex incidents, enabling faster response and adaptive defense strategies.
- Includes predefined playbooks for automatic responses to specific threats. They can start automatically from predefined rules or be triggered by the security staff.

**Why it matters:** a SIEM produces more alerts than most SecOps teams can realistically investigate. SOAR handles many of them automatically and leaves manual work for cases that really need a human, so analysts can focus on more complex and potentially more damaging threats.

## SOC Metrics
Understanding how well a SOC performs is key to improving its people, processes and technology. Many metrics or KPIs can be designed, but five are commonly used. Keep in mind that general performance metrics often don't give an accurate picture of how a SOC is really doing, because of the diversity of cybersecurity threats.

- **Dwell time:** how long threat actors have access to a network before they are detected and their access is stopped.
- **Mean Time to Detect (MTTD):** the average time it takes SOC staff to identify that valid security incidents have occurred in the network.
- **Mean Time to Respond (MTTR):** the average time it takes to stop and remediate a security incident.
- **Mean Time to Contain (MTTC):** the time needed to stop an incident from causing further damage to systems or data.
- **Time to Control:** the time needed to stop malware from spreading across the network.

## Enterprise and managed security
Medium and large networks benefit from an enterprise-level SOC. It can be fully in-house, but many large organizations outsource at least part of their SOC operations to a security provider.

As an example of what a provider can offer, Cisco has expert teams that help resolve incidents quickly and accurately, with a range of incident-response, preparation and management capabilities:
- Smart Net Total Care (fast problem resolution).
- PSIRT: incident response team for Cisco products.
- CSIRT: incident response team for computer security.
- Managed services.
- TacOps: tactical operations.
- Physical security program.

## DevSecOps
DevSecOps stands for Development, Security and Operations. It is the practice of building security into the DevOps process, so that security is part of the whole software lifecycle instead of something added at the end. By including it from the start, the goal is to reduce vulnerabilities and improve the overall security of applications, systems and environments.

What it provides:
- **Faster remediation:** security problems are found early, which lowers the time and cost of fixing them.
- **Reduced risk:** continuous security checks make it less likely that vulnerabilities reach production.
- **Greater efficiency:** automating security tasks lets teams focus on their main responsibilities while security is still covered.
- **Better collaboration:** security becomes a shared responsibility, improving communication between developers, security and operations teams.

The aim is a security culture where everyone is responsible for security at every stage of development, producing safer software, faster risk mitigation and a more resilient infrastructure.

## Security vs. availability
Most enterprise networks must be up all the time, so security staff know that keeping the network available is key to meeting the organization's priorities.

Each business has a limited tolerance for downtime, usually based on comparing the cost of downtime with the cost of protecting against it. For example, a small shop with one location may be fine with a single router as its only point of failure. But if most of its sales come from online customers, the owner may decide to add redundancy so there is always a connection.

Uptime is usually measured as the amount of downtime per year, expressed in "nines":

| Availability | Downtime per year |
|---|---|
| 99.8% | 17.52 hours |
| 99.9% ("three nines") | 8.76 hours |
| 99.99% ("four nines") | 52.56 minutes |
| 99.999% ("five nines") | 5.256 minutes |
| 99.9999% ("six nines") | 31.56 seconds |
| 99.99999% ("seven nines") | 3.16 seconds |

**Key idea:** the level of security must not be so high that it gets in the way of employees' needs or business functions. The goal is a balance between good security and the company being able to operate effectively.
